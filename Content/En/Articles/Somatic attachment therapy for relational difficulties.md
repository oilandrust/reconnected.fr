# How somatic attachment therapy can help with relational difficulties
date: 20 November 2026
datetime: 2026-11-20

Somatic attachment therapy brings awareness to our unconscious relational patterns and helps transform them gently. These attachment patterns live in the nervous system and in the way the body responds to intimacy, in order to protect us from old wounds. By bringing mindfulness and a new experience of safety in connection, somatic therapy can soften those responses.

## Why relationships keep repeating the same patterns

Relational difficulties are often linked to our wounds, whatever their origin — past experiences in adult life or during childhood. To protect ourselves from living those wounds again, we adapt in our relationships. We may become anxious and seek contact at all costs if we fear abandonment, or, conversely, withdraw and seek more distance if past relationships felt smothering.

These two positions can relate to an anxious or avoidant attachment style, and most people show tendencies in both directions.

## Mechanisms that often stay unconscious

Whatever strategy we use to avoid being hurt, those mechanisms — and the wounds they protect — are often unconscious. That is why change is hard, even when we can see our patterns.

These unconscious responses to closeness are recorded in the nervous system and in the body. The system may become hypersensitive to abandonment; that shows up as anxiety and tension, and sometimes as clinging impulses. These phenomena can be studied: the vulnerable parts and their needs can become conscious. When presence is brought to those parts, they soften, and our responses to intimacy in everyday life become more flexible.

## How Hakomi works with attachment

In Hakomi therapy, we take time to study our habitual responses and explore how they organise themselves through small experiments. For example, we may look at how a need is expressed, or how the body reacts when we become close. Mindful exploration opens the possibility of a new experience of safety in connection — and, gradually, of more flexible and authentic relationships.
